# Write in the reader's English

Write directly in English from the task, facts and audience. Use frameworks to reason about the offer, not to imitate an expert's personality. A framework's example is not evidence about the user's product.

## Find the voice

Use approved brand examples to identify rhythm, vocabulary, warmth, humour and how directly the brand sells. They are style references, not product facts or sentences to copy. Without examples, choose clear, human language appropriate to the situation.

Use the audience's English variety where specified. Do not assume that English means a US audience, aggressive sales language or a particular currency. If the market materially changes the wording and is unknown, ask. Keep regional spelling consistent.

## Build the message

- Explain what the reader gets, why it matters and what the next action actually does.
- Prefer concrete verbs and details over abstract claims. Keep personality when it helps the reader.
- Write natural sentences rather than translating Swedish word order or idioms literally.
- Use established industry terms when the audience uses them. Explain unfamiliar terms where needed.
- Preserve prices, currency, tax treatment, dates, units, deadlines, guarantees and other terms. Never convert currency or silently alter a condition to localise the copy.
- Preserve required wording exactly, even when it is in another language. Flag the language mismatch and ask before translating legally or commercially significant terms.
- Keep quotations, translations and paraphrases distinct. Do not invent customer language, testimonials, evidence or urgency.

## Cut the AI patterns

AI-assisted drafts pick up the same tics. Look for them and make the minimum effective edit; keep whatever sounds like the brand.

- Empty words: delve, leverage, utilize, seamless, robust, cutting-edge, game changer, transformative, elevate, unlock your potential, in today's fast-paced world, your journey, navigate the landscape. Write what actually happens.
- False contrasts: "It's not X, it's Y" and "not just X but Y". Say Y.
- Throat-clearing and stage directions: "Here's the thing", "Let's dive in", "It's worth noting", "The key point is". Lead with the point and let facts show what matters.
- Colon reveals and drama: "The best part: …", "Not X. Not Y. Z.", stacked fragments and closing lines that try to sound profound. Write full sentences and end on the concrete point or next step.
- Clusters of em dashes, decorative bold and emoji in headings.
- Weasel attribution: "experts agree", "studies show". Name the source or cut the claim.

The portability test: if a sentence would fit unchanged at another company, it's probably filler. These patterns say nothing about who wrote a piece; they are only reasons to read it again. Based on Peter Yang's [no-ai-slop](https://github.com/petergyang/no-ai-slop) (MIT).

## Check the finished text

1. Does it answer the reader's actual question?
2. Does the voice fit this brand and audience?
3. Is the wording idiomatic, with consistent spelling and terminology?
4. Are the facts, terms and degree of certainty intact?
5. Does the call to action describe what happens next?

Revise the actual text before returning it. Do not treat word lists or automated checks as proof of writing quality or sales performance. These are our editorial guidelines, not claims of measured conversion results.
